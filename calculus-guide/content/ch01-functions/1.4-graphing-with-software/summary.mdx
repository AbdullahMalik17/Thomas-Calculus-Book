## 1. Viewing Windows and Digital Artifacts

A graphing display presents a window defined by $[x_{\min}, x_{\max}]$ by $[y_{\min}, y_{\max}]$.

### The Anatomy of Graphical Distortion
1. **Aspect Ratio Distortion**: If the visual distance per unit along the horizontal axis differs from that along the vertical axis, circles appear as ellipses, and perpendicular lines appear oblique.
2. **Hidden Local Extrema**: If the viewing window is too narrow or too wide, turning points and local roots can fall entirely outside the display area.
3. **Aliasing and Sampling Errors**: Graphing software evaluates a function at a finite number of equally spaced pixel columns. If a function oscillates rapidly (such as $f(x) = \sin(50x)$ or $f(x) = \sin(1/x)$ near $x = 0$), the plotted curve connects disjoint points arbitrarily, creating deceptive moiré patterns.
4. **Discontinuity Segment Artifacts**: In non-adaptive plotters, a function with a vertical asymptote like $y = \frac{1}{x - 2}$ may display a nearly vertical line connecting the extreme positive and negative values across the asymptote.

## 2. Parametric Equations: Curves in Motion

Many curves in physics and engineering cannot be expressed as a single function $y = f(x)$ because they double back and fail the Vertical Line Test.

### Defining a Parametric Curve
We express both $x$ and $y$ as functions of an independent parameter $t$:
$$x = f(t), \qquad y = g(t), \qquad t \in [a, b]$$

As $t$ increases from $a$ to $b$, the point $(x(t), y(t))$ traces out a curve with a specific **orientation** (direction of motion).

### Eliminating the Parameter
To uncover the Cartesian equation:
1. **Algebraic Substitution**: Solve one equation for $t$ (e.g. $t = \frac{x - x_0}{v_x}$) and substitute into the second equation.
2. **Trigonometric Elimination**: For circular or elliptical trajectories involving sine and cosine, apply the Pythagorean identity:
   $$x = h + a\cos t, \quad y = k + b\sin t \implies \frac{(x - h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y - k)^2}{b^2} = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$$

## 3. Empirical Modeling and Logarithmic Scales

When working with experimental data, transforming variables linearizes power and exponential laws:

1. **Power Function Models**: $y = a x^b$
   Taking natural logarithms of both sides yields:
   $$\ln y = \ln a + b \ln x$$
   Plotting $\ln y$ versus $\ln x$ (a **log-log plot**) produces a straight line whose slope is the power $b$ and whose vertical intercept is $\ln a$.

2. **Exponential Models**: $y = a e^{kx}$
   Taking natural logarithms:
   $$\ln y = \ln a + kx$$
   Plotting $\ln y$ versus $x$ (a **semi-log plot**) produces a straight line whose slope is the growth/decay rate $k$.

## 4. Common Student Pitfalls

1. **Ignoring Parametric Orientation and Domain Truncation**:
   Eliminating $t$ from $x = t^2, y = t$ yields $x = y^2$. However, because $x = t^2 \ge 0$, the parametric curve only traces the right half of the parabola ($x \ge 0$). Failing to retain parameter restrictions leads to incorrect geometric domains.
2. **Confusing Window Bounds with Domain/Range**:
   Setting $[x_{\min}, x_{\max}] = [-10, 10]$ on a grapher does not change the mathematical domain of $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$; the display simply leaves $x < 0$ blank.
